Australia is home to over 700 species of birds , of these species about 350 are only found in Australia, such as the emu, noisy miner and little raven. In this data visualisation we look at the birds recorded on the Atlas of Living Australia in Victoria, mainly in Greater Melbourne. We explore where they are reported, what is reported, what affects these sightings, and who reports them.

We will be taking a look into birds recorded through apps such as eBird and iNaturalist, and stored on the Atlas of Living Australia. These records exist thanks to keen birdwatchers and nature lovers who note down the birds they see. So keep in mind that this is not a picture of every bird out there. Yet it is still fascinating to dive into the world of our feathered neighbours. Let’s zoom into Melbourne and see which suburbs record the most birds for their population.

We’ve seen where Melbourne’s birds get recorded. Now let’s look at which birds people are recording.

Which birds do we record?

Out of 327 different birds recorded in Melbourne, just 10 make up over a quarter of all records. They’re the birds we see every day: in our backyards, on our streets and in our parks. Two birds on the list aren’t even native to Australia. The Common Myna (7th), from South and Southeast Asia, and the Spotted Dove (11th), also from southern Asia, were both brought to Melbourne in the 1860s.

Follow the coloured lines: each bird is coloured by the season it ranks highest in. The Common Myna peaks in summer, the Red Wattlebird in spring and the Little Raven in winter. Only the magpie never moves, sitting at the top all year round.

Some of these birds travel huge distances. Latham’s Snipe breeds in Japan and flies to Australia for our summer, while the Sacred Kingfisher heads north for winter. Meanwhile, robins come down from the hills in autumn, and winter birds like the Powerful Owl are around all year but easier to spot when the trees are bare.

What else impacts bird observations?

80% of bird observations were recorded within 10 metres of a tree canopy while only 48% of the city is within 10 metres of a tree

Nearly 3 in 10 records were made right under a tree, even though trees cover just 11% of the city.

The biggest circles sit on the leafiest parks: Royal Park, Carlton Gardens, Fitzroy Gardens and the Botanic Gardens

Most of the observations come from two big parks, Royal Park and the Botanic Gardens, which hold 42% of the City’s trees but 69% of its bird records. Parks offer paths, water and visitors as well as trees, so this shows where birds are noticed.

Who reports the birds?

You might expect bird records to be spread fairly evenly across the people who make them.

They aren’t. Of the 2,509 people who logged birds in Melbourne on iNaturalist in 2024, just 37 made half of all records, and one person alone made almost 1 in 10.

Disclosure: Uses iNaturalist only, the one source that shows who made each record.

Birds are usually most active and noisy around dawn, but almost nobody records then.

Only 1.2% of records were made before 7am, while 73% were made between 9am and 5pm. Sundays were the busiest day, and an average weekend day had 23% more records than a weekday.

Although the brightest square is on Monday at noon by mostly one person: they made 421 of its 987 records.

Your turn

These records are made by everyday people, and you can be one of them. Whether you’re a keen birdwatcher or just out for a stroll, log the birds you see on apps like eBird or iNaturalist. Every record helps scientists track how our birds are doing. Together, these records show which birds are becoming rarer, where they live, and how they’re shifting as our cities and climate change. The records help decide which birds need protecting and which places matter most to them. Scientists can’t be everywhere, so we need to help fill in the gaps, from backyards to suburbs that rarely get counted.

Notes on the data

  • All bird records are from calendar year 2024, the latest full year: eBird's 2025 records in the Atlas stop in August.
  • A record is one sighting logged by one person, not one bird. eBird makes one record per species per checklist.
  • State rates use the population at 30 June 2024. Suburb rates use the 2021 Census, the latest suburb populations; suburbs with fewer than 1,000 residents are greyed out.
  • The tree, observer and time charts use iNaturalist records only: eBird places records at hotspot pins rather than where the bird was, and only iNaturalist shares who made a record and when. Observers are anonymous.
  • Tree cover is from 2021 aerial imagery, the newest available; bird records are from 2024.
